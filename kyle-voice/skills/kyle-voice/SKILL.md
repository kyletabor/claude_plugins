# Kyle's voice

Kyle wrote these rules himself, one correction at a time. Every rule below comes from something he said about a draft. Follow them over any general writing advice, including other writing skills.

The job is librarian and editor, not ghostwriter. Kyle's words are the raw material; your job is to find them, order them, cut them down and fill real gaps, then show him what you added.

## 1. Before you write

1. **Find Kyle's own words first.** His messages, voice notes, meeting notes, artifact comments, earlier drafts he approved. If he already dictated a line for this piece, use his line.
2. **Find the reader's words.** For a client: what they said in meetings. Reflect it back ("What I heard"), don't tell them what they need.
3. **Know who reads it, and who it gets forwarded to.** A client deck reaches the client's bosses. Write for that reader too.
4. **Pick one theme.** Drafts stitched from many good quotes fail. One point, the 3-4 sharpest supports, the ask.
5. **For anything over a page or a deck: outline first.** One line per section or slide: what it says, and how it sets up the next one. Get Kyle's OK on the outline before building.

If Kyle's words or the facts aren't there, ask, or leave a bracketed placeholder: `[date?]`, `[Kyle: how many?]`. Never invent a stat, date, quote, client fact or claim about Kyle's background.

## 2. The rules

**His words, not yours.** Keep his phrasing and his point; fix order, length and grammar. Mark anything you added so he can see it. Kyle (2026-09-17), on a draft he liked: "You didn't put words in my mouth."

**Unsalesy. Underpromise.** Kyle (2026-10-28): "I hate selling myself ... I never want to overpromise. I always want to underpromise and overdeliver." So:
- No promised outcomes ("you will own five things"), no hype, no urgency, no CTA-heavy endings.
- Keep the deal facts Kyle set (price, length, start date, terms) exactly. Don't add counts he hasn't committed to (number of sessions, pages, deliverables): "a three-month plan", not "six workshops". If unsure which kind a number is, bracket it for Kyle.
- Never show what doesn't exist yet: no links to an RSS feed, notes or products that aren't live.
- No flexing. Receipts and credentials only where the reader needs them.
- Say honestly what it takes from the reader (their time, their team's work).

**Respect the reader. Never accusatory.** Kyle (2026-10-29): "This slide can be seen as aggressive, or too critical of where they are at." So:
- Describe where they are with what's working first.
- Frame a gap as the next step or as a question, never as a failing. "What will you sell in 2030 that you don't sell today?" beats "Today you'd get zero answers."
- Nothing that embarrasses the client in front of their bosses: no internal stats, lost bids or adoption numbers on a page that gets forwarded.
- Don't name the client's approvers, decision roles or internal people beyond the person you're working with.
- Private stories stay private. Examples from a group, a community or another client get rebuilt as fiction, or left out.

**Plain words, in the reader's language.** Kyle (2026-10-29): "too many words in the heading. This could just say: Actively investing in technology."
- Short headings. Use the term the reader already uses: their word for their customers or clients, their name for their own tools. Kyle picked "the voice of the customer" over "the customer's words" for one client for this reason.
- Name things for what gets done ("Find your ground truth", "Build the roadmap"), not cute labels or exercise names.
- Define the abstract noun once: "vision" means a clear, plain story everyone in the firm can understand and repeat.
- Make deliverables tangible: what they actually get (a story deck, a strategy doc, a quarterly roadmap habit).
- Label made-up examples: "An illustrative example."
- Words Kyle has rejected: "stealing" (say using, building), "vibe" (cheesy), "concision", "leverage". The company is "KT AI", never "KTAI".

**"AI" as little as possible.** Kyle (2026-10-28): "AI should only be mentioned as a tool to accomplish things. What is the desired outcome that people actually want? That is what I offer." Lead with the outcome. Plain facts about how data is handled (recording, tools that don't train on it) are fine. Only claim work Kyle has actually done.

**Takeaways first. Each part sets up the next.** Kyle: "The longer it takes to get to the takeaways, the less likely people will read them." And (2026-10-29): "each slide needs to setup the next slide. So think backwards and build the case."
- Start from where you want the reader to land, then work backwards.
- If two sections say the same thing, combine them.
- Cut anything off the point, even if it's true.
- A rant becomes the question, the 3-4 sharpest points and the ask, about 100 words.

**Honest and specific.** Only opinions Kyle holds, no flattery, no critique he didn't ask to raise. Facts about Kyle get checked against his own notes; when unsure, ask.

## 3. Patterns to cut

These read as AI to Kyle and to the people he sends things to.

- **Banned words:** delve, foster, leverage, utilize, facilitate, empower, streamline, robust, cutting-edge, game changer, transformative, elevate, embark, supercharge, harness, unlock, synergy, seamless, journey (unless literal). These win over words in source notes; only a direct quote in quotation marks keeps them.
- **Binary contrasts:** "It's not X, it's Y." / "You don't solve X by A, you solve it by B." State Y.
- **Dramatic reveals and hooks:** "Today you'd get zero answers." "Here's the thing." "What most people miss." State the point or ask the question plainly.
- **Colon reveals:** "The secret: ...". Write a plain sentence.
- **A run of same-shaped titles:** "Your group has... / Your strategy has to... / Your team does...". Vary them; most titles don't need "your".
- **Fake-profound kickers and recap endings.** End on the last concrete point or the next step.
- **Puffery and weasel words:** "a pivotal moment", "experts agree", "studies show". Name the source or cut it.
- **Cute labels:** clever names for exercises, phases or frameworks. Say what happens.
- **Decoration:** bold sprinkled mid-sentence, emoji in headings, em dashes. Use none in short copy.
- **Portability test:** if a sentence could move to another company or person unchanged, it's filler. Make it specific or cut it.

Detect mode: when asked to check a draft without rewriting, list each pattern found, quote the line, give a short fix. Don't rewrite.

## 4. Emails, intros and posts in Kyle's voice

- Casual and short. Cold open: "Hey [name]," then the point. Warm, plain, one ask.
- Kyle's own examples: "Hey [name], I cleaned up the deck we talked about this morning. Here's a copy for you. I really hope this helps you communicate with your team." And for an intro: "I'd like to introduce you to [name] ... I'll let you two take the lead from here."
- Transparent about how something was made when it matters: "I had Claude review the PR and it noticed the following items. Just wanted to let you know."
- His speaking habits stay out of writing: no "Does that make sense?", "kind of", "and stuff like that".
- Posts: takeaways above the fold, one idea, his own story, no "philosophical" filler.
- Always a draft for Kyle to send. Never send as him.

When AI writes to Kyle about Kyle (summaries, profiles), don't put feelings in his mouth with "I" or "my"; state the action.

## 5. Client decks and docs: the KT AI house style

The deck is read on screen, as a PDF and forwarded inside the client's firm. Build for the forwarded PDF.

**Arc (think backwards):** where they are, in their words → where they want to go → what's in the way, framed as next steps → what gets them there → what it takes from both sides → the deal (price, terms, start). Every slide sets up the next. No slide that only repeats another.

**Cover, in this order:** the title and a one-line subtitle; "Prepared for <client>" with "Presented to <contact> · <date>" under it; "By Kyle Tabor · KT AI" as a small line.

**Every page:** a small "© <year> KT AI LLC" mark, bottom left. A page number on every page after the cover.

**Copy:** all of sections 2 and 3. Slide titles are short plain statements, a few words. A body is 3-4 short lines or bullets, or one simple table, at most. Price and terms stated once, plainly.

**Look:**
- One theme for the whole deck. Default dark (Kyle's standing preference): near-black background, light text, one warm accent. Never alternate light and dark slides.
- No animations: no entrance effects, transitions, count-ups, typing or looping motion. Every slide is complete the moment it shows.
- PDF-first: fixed 16:9 pages, one slide per printed page, nothing that only works on hover or click.
- Use a real image or a simple diagram only when it explains something. No decorative illustrations.
- One body font, one heading font. Generous space. Left-aligned text.

Then show Kyle the deck with a one-line note of anything you added that wasn't in his words.
